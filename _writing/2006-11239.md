DDPM trains a Markov chain to invert a fixed Gaussian noising process, and the whole thing collapses into training a network to predict the noise $\epsilon$ that was added.

## 1. The setup: modeling $p(x)$

- Primary goal: model the true data distribution $p(x)$ for images, where the dependencies are far too complex to write down by hand. 
- Core Problem: We have high-dimensional data (images) with complex dependencies. We want to model the true data distribution $p(x)$.

### The VAE Approach

1. Introduce a latent variable $z$ (lower-dimensional, interpretable).
2. Model $p(x) = \int p(x\mid z) p(z) dz$.
3. Use variational inference: introduce $q(z\mid x)$ to approximate $p(z\mid x)$.
4. Maximize the ELBO: $\mathbb{E}\_q[\log p(x\mid z)] - D\_{\text{KL}}(q(z\mid x) \| p(z))$.
5. The first term is reconstruction accuracy; the second is a complexity penalty.

Problem with VAE is that everything about an image has to pass through a single, much lower-dimensional latent. It's shaped like a bottleneck and it is indeed a bottleneck.

Reconstructions come out smooth and a little blurry, because the bottleneck throws away exactly the high-frequency detail that makes an image look real. Around 2015 the other option, GANs, produced sharper samples but traded away stability: the adversarial game is hard to train and prone to mode collapse.

That is the gap diffusion fills. Instead of compressing into one latent, it uses a *chain* of latents that each have the same dimensionality as the image. No information has to squeeze through a narrow neck, and there is no adversarial game, just a fixed noising process and a denoiser trained against it.

## 2. The diffusion move: destroy structure, learn to restore it

### The Diffusion Approach

1. Instead of one latent variable, use a chain: $x\_1, \ldots, x\_T$.
2. The forward process $q(x\_t\mid x\_{t-1})$ is fixed (no parameters). just adds Gaussian noise using the standard Guassian equations.
3. The reverse process $p\_\theta(x\_{t-1}\mid x\_t)$ is learned. denoises.
4. The variational bound decomposes into KL divergences between Gaussians, which are tractable.
5. The key insight: predicting the noise $\epsilon$ is equivalent to predicting the score, connecting to Langevin dynamics.

Concretely, the forward process turns an image into noise in many small steps. At $t=1$ the image is barely speckled; by $t=T$ nothing but the grossest statistics survive, and $x\_T$ is essentially standard Gaussian noise. The reverse process has to undo that one step at a time. The bet is that each individual denoising step is *easy enough to learn*, even though going from pure noise to an image in one shot is hopeless. The paper's Figure 2 is the clearest picture of this: the same reverse trajectory reads forwards as progressive destruction and backwards as progressive refinement.

But why the network should be trained to predict noise at all?

## 3. Why $\epsilon$? The score-function and Langevin lens

Predicting noise looks like a reparameterization trick. It is really a statement about **scores**, and the cleanest way to see that is through Langevin dynamics: the physics recipe for sampling from a distribution using only its gradient.

### 3.1 quick intro to Langevin dynamics

Langevin dynamics is a stochastic differential equation (SDE) from physics that describes the motion of a particle in a potential field with thermal noise. In statistics, it's used to sample from a probability distribution $p(x)$.

**The continuous form:**

$$dx = \frac{1}{2}\nabla_x \log p(x) \, dt + dW$$

where:

- $\nabla\_x \log p(x)$ is the **score function** (gradient of the log-density).
- $dW$ is a Wiener process (Brownian motion), the noise term.

**The physics picture.** Picture a ball rolling on a landscape whose height is $-\log p(x)$. The drift term $\frac{1}{2}\nabla\_x \log p(x)$ pushes the ball downhill in that landscape, i.e. toward higher probability. The $dW$ term is the thermal kicks. On its own the drift would slide the ball into the nearest mode and stop; on its own the noise would let it wander forever. 

I think of the two terms like accuracy and complexity penalty. Accuracy left alone would try to push all gradients towards the mean, and this would destroy the sampling variance. so the complexity penalty pulls it around the mean, this iteratively leads to the Guassian curve and more stable probability distribution to run the gradient on. Together they reach a balance: the rate at which drift carries mass out of a region equals the rate at which diffusion carries mass in.

The discrete form we implement:

$$x_{k+1} = x_k + \frac{\eta}{2}\nabla_x \log p(x_k) + \sqrt{\eta}\, z_k, \qquad z_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

where $\eta$ is the step size (learning rate).

Notice the mismatch in how the two terms scale with $\eta$. The drift is a deterministic push, so over a step of length $\eta$ it accumulates proportional to $\eta$. The noise is a sum of independent kicks whose variances add, so it accumulates proportional to $\sqrt{\eta}$; that is why the discrete noise is $\sqrt{\eta}\,z$ and not $\eta z$. Get this wrong and the sampler either freezes (noise too small) or diverges (noise too large). The step size also sets a bias-variance trade-off: too large and the discretization stops approximating the continuous SDE, so you sample from the wrong distribution; too small and consecutive samples stay correlated, so mixing is slow.

The particle drifts toward high-density regions (because $\nabla \log p$ points uphill in probability), but noise keeps it from collapsing to the mode. After many steps, the particle's position is distributed according to $p(x)$. The noise also protects us from getting stuck in local maxima, which is awesome.

[This video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=laaBLUxJUMY) has the most intuitive explanation for this.

**The problem**: Standard Langevin needs $\nabla\_x \log p(x)$, and for images we do not have it. 

**Solution**: Using the Gaussian Score trick: For a Gaussian $p(x) = \mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \sigma^2 I)$:

$$\nabla_x \log p(x) = \nabla_x\left[-\frac{\|x-\mu\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right] = -\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma^2}$$

Now apply this to the forward process $q(x\_t\mid x\_0) = \mathcal{N}(x\_t; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_t}x\_0, (1-\bar{\alpha}\_t)I)$:

$$\nabla_{x_t} \log q(x_t\mid x_0) = -\frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}$$

But recall from the reparameterization $x\_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_t}x\_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}\_t}\epsilon$:

$$x_t - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0 = \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon$$

So:

$$\boxed{\nabla_{x_t} \log q(x_t\mid x_0) = -\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}}$$

**This is the crucial identity.** The score is proportional to the noise $\epsilon$ (up to a scalar). So if a neural network predicts $\epsilon$, it's implicitly predicting the score:

$$\nabla_{x_t} \log q(x_t\mid x_0) \approx -\frac{\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}$$

### ok, now what? 

**the simplified DDPM loss (eq 14) is a denoising score matching objective in disguise.**

recap:
1. the score of the noised data is the noise up to a scalar (identity above)
2. $L\_{\text{simple}}$ trains the network to output that same $\epsilon$ from $x\_t$. 

Substitute the first into the second and the target stops looking like "the noise we happened to sample" and starts looking like "the score of the noised data distribution." Minimizing the $\epsilon$-MSE is therefore minimizing a timestep-weighted distance between the network and the score.

That's, I bellieve, the essence of denoising score matching. We cannot evaluate $\nabla\_x \log p(x)$ for the true data, but we *can* evaluate the score of a Gaussian we corrupted, and it has a closed form in terms of the noise we added. The network never sees the true score, only these corrupted-score targets, and yet the objective is the right one.

This is why $\epsilon$-prediction is not an arbitrary reparameterization. Choosing $\epsilon$ as the target is choosing to learn the score, and learning the score is what makes the sampler work.

### need for anneal

The paper's Section 3.2 says the sampling "resembles Langevin dynamics with $\epsilon\_\theta$ as a learned gradient of the data density." Here's the intuition:

**Standard Langevin dynamics** samples from a **fixed** distribution $p(x)$ by:

1. Computing the score $\nabla\_x \log p(x)$.
2. Taking a step: $x \leftarrow x + \frac{\eta}{2}\nabla\_x \log p(x) + \sqrt{\eta}z$.
3. Repeating until convergence.

**Diffusion sampling** is like running Langevin dynamics at **multiple noise levels**:

- At large $t$ (high noise), the distribution $p\_t(x)$ is nearly Gaussian, so the score is easy to estimate and the sampler makes big, coarse moves.
- At small $t$ (low noise), the distribution $p\_t(x)$ is close to the data distribution, so the score is sharp and the sampler makes fine adjustments.

This is called annealed Langevin dynamics ([Song & Ermon, 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.05600)): you start with a high-temperature (noisy) distribution and gradually cool down, using the score at each temperature. The diffusion model does exactly this, but with the temperature schedule baked into the forward process.

### DDPM sampler is a scheduled Langevin step

| Concept | Langevin Dynamics | Diffusion Sampling |
|---------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Goal | Sample from $p(x)$ | Sample from $p(x\_0)$ |
| Score | $\nabla\_x \log p(x)$ | $\nabla\_{x\_t} \log q(x\_t) \approx -\epsilon\_\theta/\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}\_t}$ |
| Step | $x \leftarrow x + \frac{\eta}{2}\nabla \log p + \sqrt{\eta}z$ | $x\_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha\_t}}(x\_t - \frac{1-\alpha\_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}\_t}}\epsilon\_\theta) + \sigma\_t z$ |
| Noise schedule | Fixed temperature | Annealed (large $t$ to small $t$) |
| Convergence | Asymptotic | Finite time ($T$ steps) |

Put the two halves together. DDPM's sampling update (the third row of the table above, and Algorithm 2) looks like this: take the current noisy $x\_t$, form a denoised mean using the predicted noise, then add a scaled Gaussian kick.

Compare that to the Langevin step. The deterministic part is a drift term: it moves the current point toward the denoised estimate implied by the predicted noise, which is the "uphill in probability" direction evaluated at noise level $t$. The appended Gaussian is the injected noise, the discretized Brownian kick. So each DDPM step is one Langevin step at one temperature, and running $t = T, \dots, 1$.

It is worth being precise about what is *not* identical to vanilla Langevin, though:

- The drift is not $\nabla \log p\_t(x)$ applied directly to $x$; it is a mean-reversion toward a denoised estimate, a preconditioned version of the same gradient.
- The noise magnitude is fixed (either $\beta\_t$ or $\tilde{\beta}\_t$), so the sampler cannot trade exploration against fidelity the way a Langevin sampler with a tunable $\eta$ can.

None of that breaks the analogy. It means DDPM is Langevin dynamics with the schedule baked in, which is exactly the design choice that made it work at scale.

## 4. Why the $\epsilon$-target is legal: the derivation

### Gaussian Distributions (Building Block)

A univariate Gaussian is fully specified by two parameters:

$$p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

For multi-dimensional data $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$, we need a mean vector $\mu \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and a covariance matrix $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$:

$$\mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\mid \Sigma\mid ^{1/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x-\mu)\right)$$

**Isotropic Gaussian:** When $\Sigma = \sigma^2 I$, the covariance is fixed and we only need to predict $\mu$. This is crucial for high-dimensional data. A full covariance matrix for $d=10{,}000$ would require $10^8$ parameters, which is impractical.

**Key operations we'll need:**

- **Product of Gaussians** (appears in Bayes' rule):

$$\mathcal{N}(x; \mu_1, \Sigma_1) \cdot \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_2, \Sigma_2) \propto \mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \Sigma)$$

where $\Sigma = (\Sigma\_1^{-1} + \Sigma\_2^{-1})^{-1}$ and $\mu = \Sigma(\Sigma\_1^{-1}\mu\_1 + \Sigma\_2^{-1}\mu\_2)$.

- **KL divergence between two Gaussians** (closed form):

$$D_{\text{KL}}(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \Sigma_1) \| \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \Sigma_2)) = \frac{1}{2}\left[\log\frac{\mid \Sigma_2\mid }{\mid \Sigma_1\mid } - d + \text{tr}(\Sigma_2^{-1}\Sigma_1) + (\mu_2-\mu_1)^\top \Sigma_2^{-1} (\mu_2-\mu_1)\right]$$

For isotropic Gaussians with $\Sigma\_1 = \sigma\_1^2 I$ and $\Sigma\_2 = \sigma\_2^2 I$:

$$D_{\text{KL}} = \frac{d}{2}\log\frac{\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2} - \frac{d}{2} + \frac{d\sigma_1^2}{2\sigma_2^2} + \frac{\|\mu_1-\mu_2\|^2}{2\sigma_2^2}$$

The Gaussian facts above are the whole toolkit. What follows is the bound that uses them.

### Variational Inference and the ELBO

**The problem:** We want to model a complex data distribution $p(x)$ using latent variables $z$:

$$p(x) = \int p(x\mid z) p(z) \, dz$$

But this integral is intractable for complex models.

**The solution:** Introduce a variational distribution $q(z\mid x)$ (the "recognition model" or "encoder") that approximates the true posterior $p(z\mid x)$. Then:

$$\log p(x) = \log \int p(x,z) \, dz = \log \int q(z\mid x) \frac{p(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)} \, dz = \log \mathbb{E}_{q(z\mid x)}\left[\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\right]$$

**Jensen's inequality:** For a concave function $f$ (like $\log$):

$$f(\mathbb{E}[X]) \geq \mathbb{E}[f(X)]$$

Since $\log$ is concave:

$$\log p(x) \geq \mathbb{E}_{q(z\mid x)}\left[\log \frac{p(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\right] =: \text{ELBO}$$

**Unpacking the ELBO:**

$$\text{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x\mid z)] - D_{\text{KL}}(q(z\mid x) \| p(z))$$

- **First term (accuracy/reconstruction):** How well the latent $z$ explains the data $x$.
- **Second term (complexity penalty):** How far the approximate posterior is from the prior.

**Why this matters for DDPM:** In a VAE, we have one latent variable $z$. In a diffusion model, we have a **chain** of latent variables $x\_1, x\_2, \ldots, x\_T$ of the same dimensionality as the data $x\_0$. The ELBO generalizes to:

$$\log p(x_0) \geq \mathbb{E}_q\left[\log \frac{p(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}\right]$$

This is the starting point for the diffusion model's variational bound.

The table below is the VAE-to-diffusion dictionary. The rest of this act is just filling in the right column.

### From VAE to Diffusion Models

| | VAE | Diffusion Model |
|---|---|---|
| Latent variables | $z$ (single, lower-dimensional) | $x\_1, \ldots, x\_T$ (chain, same dimension as data) |
| Encoder | $q(z\|x)$ (learned) | $q(x\_{1:T}\|x\_0)$ (fixed, no parameters) |
| Decoder | $p(x\|z)$ (learned) | $p\_\theta(x\_{0:T})$ (learned Markov chain) |
| Prior | $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ | $p(x\_T) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ |
| Training | Maximize ELBO | Maximize variational bound |

The crucial difference: **the forward process $q$ has no parameters**. It's a fixed Gaussian diffusion. This makes the variational bound tractable because all the posteriors $q(x\_{t-1}\mid x\_t, x\_0)$ are Gaussian.

### Forward Process (Diffusion)

**Definition (Eq. 2):**

$$q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0) := \prod_{t=1}^T q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}), \quad q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}) := \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\, x_{t-1}, \beta_t I)$$

where $\beta\_1, \ldots, \beta\_T$ is a fixed variance schedule (hyperparameters).

**Notation:** Let $\alpha\_t := 1 - \beta\_t$ and $\bar{\alpha}\_t := \prod\_{s=1}^t \alpha\_s$.

**Derivation of the closed form (Eq. 4):**

We want to show $q(x\_t\mid x\_0) = \mathcal{N}(x\_t; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_t} x\_0, (1-\bar{\alpha}\_t)I)$.

*Proof by induction:*

Base case $t=1$: $q(x\_1\mid x\_0) = \mathcal{N}(x\_1; \sqrt{\alpha\_1} x\_0, \beta\_1 I) = \mathcal{N}(x\_1; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_1} x\_0, (1-\bar{\alpha}\_1)I)$. ✓

Inductive step: Assume $q(x\_{t-1}\mid x\_0) = \mathcal{N}(x\_{t-1}; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1}} x\_0, (1-\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1})I)$.

We can write:

$$x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \epsilon_{t-1}, \quad \epsilon_{t-1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

$$x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t} x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t} \epsilon_t, \quad \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

Substituting:

$$x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \epsilon_{t-1}) + \sqrt{\beta_t} \epsilon_t$$

$$= \sqrt{\alpha_t \bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} x_0 + \sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})} \epsilon_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t} \epsilon_t$$

The sum of two independent Gaussians is Gaussian:

$$\sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})} \epsilon_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t} \epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, [\alpha_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}) + \beta_t] I)$$

Now, $\alpha\_t(1-\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1}) + \beta\_t = \alpha\_t - \alpha\_t\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1} + 1 - \alpha\_t = 1 - \alpha\_t\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1} = 1 - \bar{\alpha}\_t$.

Also, $\sqrt{\alpha\_t \bar{\alpha}\_{t-1}} = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_t}$.

Therefore:

$$q(x_t\mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0, (1-\bar{\alpha}_t)I) \quad \blacksquare$$

**Reparameterization:** We can write $x\_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_t} x\_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}\_t} \epsilon$ where $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.

**Why this matters:** We can sample $x\_t$ at any timestep $t$ directly from $x\_0$, without simulating the entire chain. This makes training efficient.

### Reverse Process

**Definition (Eq. 1):**

$$p_\theta(x_{0:T}) := p(x_T) \prod_{t=1}^T p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t), \quad p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t) := \mathcal{N}(x_{t-1}; \mu_\theta(x_t, t), \Sigma_\theta(x_t, t))$$

where $p(x\_T) = \mathcal{N}(x\_T; 0, I)$.

The reverse process is a **learned** Markov chain that gradually denoises $x\_T$ back to $x\_0$.

**Why Gaussian transitions?** When each $\beta\_t$ is small, the forward process takes such tiny steps that its transition kernel is barely different from the reverse one, and both land in the same functional family (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015). Concretely: a small Gaussian step is approximately invertible by another Gaussian step, so it is reasonable to *assume* the reverse conditionals are Gaussian and let the network learn their means. The assumption is not exact, but the error shrinks as $T$ grows, which is why the theory is usually stated in the continuous-time limit.

**Simplification:** The authors set $\Sigma\_\theta(x\_t, t) = \sigma\_t^2 I$ (untrained time-dependent constants). They tried both $\sigma\_t^2 = \beta\_t$ and $\sigma\_t^2 = \tilde{\beta}\_t = \frac{1-\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}\_t}\beta\_t$ and found similar results. The first is optimal for $x\_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, the second for $x\_0$ deterministically set to one point.

So the only learned part is the mean $\mu\_\theta(x\_t, t)$.

### Variational Bound (ELBO for Diffusion)

**Starting point:**

$$\mathbb{E}[-\log p_\theta(x_0)] \leq \mathbb{E}_q\left[-\log \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}\right] =: L$$

**Derivation (Eq. 3 → Eq. 5):**

$$L = \mathbb{E}_q\left[-\log p(x_T) - \sum_{t=1}^T \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)}{q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})}\right]$$

Separate the $t=1$ term:

$$L = \mathbb{E}_q\left[-\log p(x_T) - \sum_{t=2}^T \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)}{q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})} - \log \frac{p_\theta(x_0\mid x_1)}{q(x_1\mid x_0)}\right]$$

Use Bayes' rule on the forward process:

$$q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}) = \frac{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) q(x_t\mid x_0)}{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)}$$

This holds because $q(x\_t\mid x\_{t-1}, x\_0) = q(x\_t\mid x\_{t-1})$ (Markov property).

Substituting:

$$\log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)}{q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})} = \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)}{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)} - \log q(x_t\mid x_0) + \log q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)$$

Summing over $t=2$ to $T$:

$$\sum_{t=2}^T \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)}{q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})} = \sum_{t=2}^T \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)}{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)} - \sum_{t=2}^T \log q(x_t\mid x_0) + \sum_{t=2}^T \log q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)$$

The telescoping sums: $-\sum\_{t=2}^T \log q(x\_t\mid x\_0) + \sum\_{t=2}^T \log q(x\_{t-1}\mid x\_0) = -\log q(x\_T\mid x\_0) + \log q(x\_1\mid x\_0)$.

Putting it all together:

$$L = \mathbb{E}_q\left[-\log \frac{p(x_T)}{q(x_T\mid x_0)} - \sum_{t=2}^T \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)}{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)} - \log p_\theta(x_0\mid x_1)\right]$$

$$= \mathbb{E}_q[\underbrace{D_{\text{KL}}(q(x_T\mid x_0) \| p(x_T))}_{L_T} + \sum_{t=2}^T \underbrace{D_{\text{KL}}(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) \| p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t))}_{L_{t-1}} \underbrace{- \log p_\theta(x_0\mid x_1)}_{L_0}]$$

This is **Eq. 5**.

**Interpretation:**

- $L\_T$: Constant during training (forward process has no parameters, $p(x\_T) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$).
- $L\_{t-1}$: KL divergence between the forward process posterior and the reverse process. Both are Gaussians, so this has a closed form.
- $L\_0$: Reconstruction term (discrete decoder).

### Forward Process Posterior

Deriving the Forward Process Posterior $q(x\_{t-1}\mid x\_t, x\_0)$

We want to find the distribution of $x\_{t-1}$ given **both** $x\_t$ (the noisier version) and $x\_0$ (the clean original)(forward process posterior).

Both are Gaussians, so the KL divergence has a closed form. But to write it down, **we need the mean and variance of $q(x\_{t-1}\mid x\_t, x\_0)$**.

#### Step 1: Bayes' Rule

We want $q(x\_{t-1}\mid x\_t, x\_0)$. By Bayes:

$$q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) = \frac{q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}, x_0) \, q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)}{q(x_t\mid x_0)}$$

Because of the Markov property, $q(x\_t\mid x\_{t-1}, x\_0) = q(x\_t\mid x\_{t-1})$. So:

$$q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) = \frac{q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}) \, q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)}{q(x_t\mid x_0)}$$

We know all three pieces from the forward process definitions:

| Term | Distribution |
|------|-------------|
| $q(x\_t\|x\_{t-1})$ | $\mathcal{N}(x\_t; \sqrt{\alpha\_t}\, x\_{t-1}, \beta\_t I)$ |
| $q(x\_{t-1}\|x\_0)$ | $\mathcal{N}(x\_{t-1}; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1}}\, x\_0, (1-\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1}) I)$ |
| $q(x\_t\|x\_0)$ | $\mathcal{N}(x\_t; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_t}\, x\_0, (1-\bar{\alpha}\_t) I)$ |

The denominator $q(x\_t\mid x\_0)$ does **not** depend on $x\_{t-1}$, so it acts as a normalizing constant when we view the result as a function of $x\_{t-1}$. This means:

$$q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) \propto \underbrace{q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})}_{\text{as a function of } x_{t-1}} \cdot q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)$$

**Key trick:** We must rewrite $q(x\_t\mid x\_{t-1})$ as a function of $x\_{t-1}$, not $x\_t$. This is where the "quadratic in $x\_{t-1}$" comes from.

#### Step 2: Write Both Gaussians as Functions of $x\_{t-1}$

##### First factor: $q(x\_t\mid x\_{t-1})$ viewed as a function of $x\_{t-1}$

$$q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi\beta_t)^{d/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{\|x_t - \sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_{t-1}\|^2}{2\beta_t}\right)$$

As a function of $x\_{t-1}$, the exponential is:

$$-\frac{1}{2\beta_t}\|x_t - \sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_{t-1}\|^2 = -\frac{\alpha_t}{2\beta_t}\left\|x_{t-1} - \frac{x_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\right\|^2 + \text{const}(x_t)$$

So in precision form, the **precision** (inverse variance) contributed by this factor is:

$$\text{precision}_1 = \frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t}$$

And the **mean** of this factor (in $x\_{t-1}$) is $\frac{x\_t}{\sqrt{\alpha\_t}}$.

##### Second factor: $q(x\_{t-1}\mid x\_0)$

$$q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0) = \frac{1}{(2\pi(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}))^{d/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{\|x_{t-1} - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\, x_0\|^2}{2(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}\right)$$

The **precision** contributed by this factor is:

$$\text{precision}_2 = \frac{1}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}$$

And the **mean** is $\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1}}\, x\_0$.

#### Step 3: Multiply Two Gaussians

**Rule:** If $p\_1(x) = \mathcal{N}(x; \mu\_1, \sigma\_1^2 I)$ and $p\_2(x) = \mathcal{N}(x; \mu\_2, \sigma\_2^2 I)$, then:

$$p_1(x) \cdot p_2(x) \propto \mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \sigma^2 I)$$

where:

$$\frac{1}{\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma_1^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_2^2} \quad \text{(precisions add)}$$

$$\mu = \sigma^2 \left(\frac{\mu_1}{\sigma_1^2} + \frac{\mu_2}{\sigma_2^2}\right)$$

This is the standard "product of Gaussians" result. Intuitively: if two sources of information both constrain $x$, the combined precision is the sum of individual precisions, and the combined mean is a precision-weighted average of the individual means.

**Applying this:**

Precision of the product:

$$\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_t} = \underbrace{\frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t}}_{\text{from } q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}}_{\text{from } q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)}$$

This is exactly the expression in the paper.

#### Step 4: Simplify the Variance

$$\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_t} = \frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t} + \frac{1}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}$$

Put over a common denominator:

$$= \frac{\alpha_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}) + \beta_t}{\beta_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}$$

Now use the identity: $\alpha\_t(1-\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1}) + \beta\_t = \alpha\_t - \alpha\_t\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1} + 1 - \alpha\_t = 1 - \alpha\_t\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1}$

And since $\bar{\alpha}\_t = \alpha\_t \bar{\alpha}\_{t-1}$, we have $1 - \alpha\_t\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1} = 1 - \bar{\alpha}\_t$. So:

$$\frac{1}{\tilde{\beta}_t} = \frac{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}{\beta_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{\tilde{\beta}_t = \frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \beta_t}$$

#### Step 5: Simplify the Mean

The mean of the product is:

$$\tilde{\mu}_t = \tilde{\beta}_t \left(\frac{\mu_1}{\sigma_1^2} + \frac{\mu_2}{\sigma_2^2}\right) = \tilde{\beta}_t \left(\frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t} \cdot \frac{x_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} + \frac{1}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} \cdot \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\, x_0\right)$$

Simplify the first term: $\frac{\alpha\_t}{\beta\_t} \cdot \frac{x\_t}{\sqrt{\alpha\_t}} = \frac{\sqrt{\alpha\_t}}{\beta\_t} x\_t$.

So:

$$\tilde{\mu}_t = \tilde{\beta}_t \left(\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{\beta_t} x_t + \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} x_0\right)$$

Now substitute $\tilde{\beta}\_t = \frac{1-\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}\_t} \beta\_t$:

**Coefficient of $x\_0$:**

$$\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \beta_t \cdot \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}} = \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\, \beta_t}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}$$

**Coefficient of $x\_t$:**

$$\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \beta_t \cdot \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}{\beta_t} = \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}$$

So:

$$\boxed{\tilde{\mu}_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\, \beta_t}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} x_t}$$

#### Step 6: Assemble the Result

$$q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t-1}; \tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0), \tilde{\beta}_t I)$$

with $\tilde{\mu}\_t$ and $\tilde{\beta}\_t$ as above. This is **Eq. 6–7**.

Once we have $\tilde{\mu}\_t$ and $\tilde{\beta}\_t$, the KL divergence $L\_{t-1} = D\_{\text{KL}}(q(x\_{t-1}\mid x\_t,x\_0) \| p\_\theta(x\_{t-1}\mid x\_t))$ is a comparison between two Gaussians. Both with the same variance (since $p\_\theta$ uses fixed $\sigma\_t^2 I$). The KL simplifies to a scaled MSE between the means:

$$L_{t-1} = \frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\|\tilde{\mu}_t - \mu_\theta(x_t,t)\|^2 + C$$

Then, by reparameterizing $\tilde{\mu}\_t$ in terms of the noise $\epsilon$, we get the simplified objective $L\_{\text{simple}}$. The whole derivation hinges on this forward posterior being Gaussian, which is why the Gaussian forward process is so convenient.

### Parameterization and Simplified Loss

**Goal:** Parameterize $\mu\_\theta(x\_t, t)$ to predict $\tilde{\mu}\_t(x\_t, x\_0)$.

#### Starting Point: Eq. (8)

The paper's Eq. (8) is:

$$L_{t-1} = \mathbb{E}_q\left[\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\|\tilde{\mu}_t(x_t, x_0) - \mu_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2\right] + C$$

where $C$ is a constant independent of $\theta$ (it comes from the entropy of the Gaussian, which doesn't depend on $\theta$).

#### Step 1: Reparameterize $x\_t$

The forward process gives:

$$x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

This is a deterministic function of $x\_0$ and $\epsilon$. So we can change variables in the expectation:

$$\mathbb{E}_{q(x_t, x_0)}[\cdots] = \mathbb{E}_{x_0, \epsilon}[\cdots]$$

because $x\_t$ is fully determined by $x\_0$ and $\epsilon$.

#### Step 2: Express $\tilde{\mu}\_t$ in Terms of $x\_t$ and $x\_0$

From Eq. (7):

$$\tilde{\mu}_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\beta_t}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}x_t$$

This depends on both $x\_0$ and $x\_t$.

#### Step 3: Invert the Reparameterization to Get $x\_0$

From $x\_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_t}\,x\_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}\_t}\,\epsilon$, solve for $x\_0$:

$$x_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}}\left(x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon\right)$$

#### Step 4: Substitute $x\_0$ into $\tilde{\mu}\_t$

$$\tilde{\mu}_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\beta_t}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}}\left(x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon\right) + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}x_t$$

Now use $\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_t} = \sqrt{\alpha\_t \bar{\alpha}\_{t-1}}$, so $\frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_{t-1}}}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_t}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha\_t}}$.

First term:

$$\frac{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}}\beta_t}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}}\left(x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon\right) = \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)}\left(x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon\right)$$

$$= \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)}x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon$$

Second term:

$$\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}x_t$$

#### Step 5: Combine the $x\_t$ Terms

$$\tilde{\mu}_t = \left[\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)} + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\right]x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon$$

Put the $x\_t$ coefficient over a common denominator:

$$\frac{\beta_t + \alpha_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1})}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)}$$

Now use the identity:

$$\beta_t + \alpha_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_{t-1}) = (1-\alpha_t) + \alpha_t - \alpha_t\bar{\alpha}_{t-1} = 1 - \alpha_t\bar{\alpha}_{t-1} = 1 - \bar{\alpha}_t$$

So the coefficient simplifies to:

$$\frac{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}$$

Therefore:

$$\tilde{\mu}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon\right)$$

#### Step 6: Plug into Eq. (8)

Now substitute this expression for $\tilde{\mu}\_t$ back into Eq. (8):

$$L_{t-1} - C = \mathbb{E}_{x_0, \epsilon}\left[\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon\right) - \mu_\theta(x_t, t)\right\|^2\right]$$

This is **Eq. (10)**.

The whole point of the reparameterization in Steps 2–4 is to express $\tilde{\mu}\_t$ **entirely in terms of $x\_t$ and $\epsilon$** (not $x\_0$). This is what allows us to:

1. Write the loss as a function of $\epsilon$ (the noise) rather than $x\_0$ (the data).
2. Then substitute the parameterization $\mu\_\theta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha\_t}}(x\_t - \frac{\beta\_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}\_t}}\epsilon\_\theta)$ so that the $x\_t$ cancels and we're left with a simple MSE on $\epsilon$.

Without this reparameterization, we'd be stuck comparing $\tilde{\mu}\_t$ (which depends on $x\_0$) to $\mu\_\theta$ (which depends on $x\_t$), and the cancellation wouldn't happen.

### Deriving Eq. (12)-(14) from Eq. (10)

#### Starting Point: Eq. (10)

The paper's Eq. (10) is:

$$L_{t-1} - C = \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\epsilon}\left[\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(x_t(x_0,\epsilon) - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon\right) - \mu_\theta(x_t(x_0,\epsilon), t)\right\|^2\right]$$

where $x\_t(x\_0,\epsilon) = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_t}\,x\_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}\_t}\,\epsilon$.

#### Step 1: Substitute the Parameterization (Eq. 11)

The parameterization is:

$$\mu_\theta(x_t,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\right)$$

Substitute this into Eq. (10):

$$L_{t-1} - C = \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\epsilon}\left[\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon\right) - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\right)\right\|^2\right]$$

#### Step 2: Factor Out $1/\sqrt{\alpha\_t}$

Both terms share the factor $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha\_t}}$:

$$= \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\epsilon}\left[\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left[\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon\right) - \left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\right)\right]\right\|^2\right]$$

#### Step 3: Cancel $x\_t$ Inside the Bracket

Inside the square bracket:

$$\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon\right) - \left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\right)$$

The $x\_t$ terms cancel:

$$= -\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon + \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)$$

$$= \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\left(\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t) - \epsilon\right)$$

So the norm squared becomes:

$$\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \cdot \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}\left(\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t) - \epsilon\right)\right\|^2 = \frac{\beta_t^2}{\alpha_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)}\left\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\right\|^2$$

#### Step 4: Assemble

Plug back into $L\_{t-1} - C$:

$$L_{t-1} - C = \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\epsilon}\left[\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2} \cdot \frac{\beta_t^2}{\alpha_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)}\left\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\right\|^2\right]$$

$$= \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\epsilon}\left[\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\alpha_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)}\left\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon, t)\right\|^2\right]$$

This is **Eq. (12)**.

#### Step 5: Drop the Weighting to Get Eq. (14)

Eq. (12) has a **weight** in front:

$$w_t := \frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\alpha_t(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)}$$

This weight depends on $t$. The paper's simplification is to **set $w\_t = 1$** for all $t$, giving:

$$L_{\text{simple}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,x_0,\epsilon}\left[\left\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon, t)\right\|^2\right]$$

where now $t$ is sampled uniformly from $\{1,\ldots,T\}$.

The weighting $\frac{\beta\_t^2}{2\sigma\_t^2 \alpha\_t (1-\bar{\alpha}\_t)}$ down-weights small $t$ terms. These terms train the network to denoise with very small amounts of noise, which is trivial. By removing this weighting, the network focuses on more difficult denoising tasks at larger $t$.

### The three equations to remember

==> **Forward process closed form (Eq. 4):**

$$q(x_t\mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0, (1-\bar{\alpha}_t)I)$$

Allows efficient training by sampling any timestep directly.

==> **Reverse process parameterization (Eq. 11):**

$$\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\left(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}} \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\right)$$

Predicts noise $\epsilon$ instead of mean, connecting to score matching.

==> **Simplified training objective (Eq. 14):**

$$L_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \epsilon}\left[\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon, t)\|^2\right]$$

Unweighted MSE on noise prediction, which empirically outperforms the full variational bound.

## 5. The weird consequences

Now that the mechanism is on the table, the strange parts of the paper make sense: why the likelihood is bad even when the samples are great, why a diffusion model is secretly an autoregressive model, and what else the same machinery can be used for.

### Limitations

**Specific limitations from the paper:**

- **Log-likelihoods are not competitive:** "our models do not have competitive log likelihoods compared to other likelihood-based models" (Section 1).
- **Sampling is slow:** T=1000 steps, each requiring a neural network evaluation.
- **Learned variances are unstable:** "learning reverse process variances... leads to unstable training and poorer sample quality" (Section 4.2).
- **No learned decoder:** "It would be straightforward to instead incorporate a more powerful decoder like a conditional autoregressive model, but we leave that to future work" (Section 3.3).
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity:** Dropout rate, learning rate, batch size were tuned for CIFAR10 and transferred to other datasets.

[Improved Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09672) solves for a bunch of these.

### Section 4.3 (Progressive Coding)

**The idea:** Section 4.3 is not a new method or architecture. It's a *theoretical lens* that interprets the diffusion model as a **progressive lossy compression scheme**. It explains why diffusion models can have poor log-likelihoods yet excellent sample quality, and it connects diffusion to autoregressive decoding.

In information theory, the negative log-likelihood of a model is exactly the number of bits needed to encode data under that model.

If your model assigns probability $p\_θ(x\_0)$ to an image $x\_0$​, then the codelength is $−log⁡p\_θ(x\_0)$ bits.

    Lower codelength = better compression = higher likelihood.

Diffusion models have a variational bound L that upper-bounds the negative log-likelihood: $−log⁡p\_θ(x\_0)≤L$

So L is a codelength for the diffusion model. Section 4.3 asks: How is this codelength distributed? And what does that tell us about the model?

**Two interpretations of the variational bound:**

The bound decomposes as (Eq. 5):

$$L = L_T + \sum_{t=2}^T L_{t-1} + L_0$$

where $L\_T$ and $L\_{t-1}$ are KL divergences (cost of encoding noisy latents) and $L\_0 = -\log p\_\theta(x\_0\mid x\_1)$ is the final reconstruction term.

*Interpretation A - Lossless code:*
- **Rate** $= L\_1 + \cdots + L\_T$ (bits to specify the noisy latent sequence)
- **Distortion** $= L\_0$ (bits to specify the clean image from $x\_1$)

For their best CIFAR10 model: rate = 1.78 bits/dim, distortion = 1.97 bits/dim, total $\approx$ 3.75 bits/dim. **More than half the codelength is spent on imperceptible details**. This explains why lossless likelihood is uncompetitive (3.75 vs. 2.80 for Sparse Transformer) yet samples look great.

*Interpretation B - Progressive lossy code:*
Algorithms 3 and 4 describe a hypothetical communication protocol. The sender transmits $x\_T, x\_{T-1}, \ldots, x\_0$ sequentially. At any intermediate timestep $t$, the receiver has partial information $x\_t$ and can form a one-step denoised estimate:

$$\hat{x}_0 = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t)}{\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}} \tag{Eq. 15}$$

This comes directly from the forward process $x\_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}\_t}\,x\_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}\_t}\,\epsilon$. If you know $x\_t$ and predict $\epsilon$ with $\epsilon\_\theta$, you can solve for $x\_0$.

- **Rate** = cumulative bits received so far (sum of KL terms up to that step)
- **Distortion** = RMSE between true $x\_0$ and estimate $\hat{x}\_0$ (on a 0–255 scale)

**The rate-distortion plot (Fig. 5):** At the very beginning, distortion is huge (RMSE $\approx$ 67). As you receive a few bits, distortion drops steeply to RMSE $\approx$ 12 (rate $\approx$ 0.12). After that, distortion drops slowly to get from RMSE 12 to 0.95, you need almost all the remaining bits (rate goes from 0.12 to 1.78). 

Figure 5 explans most it, the gist of which is: **The first few bits encode coarse structure** (pose, global colors, layout); **the remaining bits encode fine details** (texture, small edges, noise). This is exactly like progressive JPEG: you first see a blocky version, then it sharpens. 

It explains the likelihood–quality gap: diffusion models waste bits on imperceptible details, but the important perceptual information is encoded in the early, low-rate bits.

### Connection to Autoregressive Decoding

(From the Progressive coding section) autoregressive models generate one pixel at a time in a fixed coordinate order. Diffusion models generate all coordinates simultaneously but with **decreasing noise**. The "bit ordering" is by noise level, not by coordinate. The paper shows that if you set up the forward process as a *masking* process (instead of Gaussian noise), the diffusion variational bound becomes exactly the autoregressive likelihood (I tried asking AI for this, the answer it gave wasnt satisfactory, I'll still not sure how this happens mathematically.. I understand it purely on an intuition level for now). So Gaussian diffusion is a **generalization** of autoregressive decoding: it uses a bit ordering that cannot be expressed by reordering data coordinates. Gaussian noise is more natural for images than masking noise. 


It provides a unified view of diffusion models and autoregressive models, suggesting that diffusion models inherit the expressive power of autoregressive models while having better inductive biases for images.

## 6. What stuck, and what came after

DDPM's contribution is less a new objective than a demonstration that diffusion works at scale when:

1. The reverse process predicts **noise $\epsilon$** rather than the mean.
2. Training uses the **unweighted MSE** on $\epsilon$.
3. The backbone is a **U-Net with self-attention and group normalization**.
4. The noise schedule is **linear** from $10^{-4}$ to $0.02$.

Underneath, the math is a variational bound that happens to decompose into Gaussian KL terms. The $\epsilon$-parameterization is what connects that bound to denoising score matching and Langevin dynamics, and the simplified objective is what redirects capacity from easy denoising toward hard denoising.

The two limitations are the seeds of the next two years of work. **Slow sampling** motivated DDIM and the family of faster deterministic samplers, then latent diffusion and distillation. **Poor likelihoods**, and the fragility of the linear schedule, motivated learned schedules and the zero-terminal-SNR fixes that are the subject of the [next note](/writing/2305-08891/). Between them, those two threads are most of how diffusion went from a curiosity to the backbone of Stable Diffusion and DALL-E 2.
